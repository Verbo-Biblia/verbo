Jesus and the Old Testament: progressive revelation, not manipulation

One of the most repeated objections against the Christian faith accuses the authors of the New Testament of "manipulating" the Old Testament, forcing a messianic reading into texts that Judaism supposedly never interpreted that way. According to this view, if the Jews did not expect a suffering Messiah, Christians would have rewritten history to fit Jesus.

This article shows that such an accusation does not hold up, from three verifiable fronts: (1) the Bible teaches progressive revelation; (2) the oldest testimonies unambiguously confess that Jesus is the Messiah; and (3) the criteria of historical reliability explain why differences between Gospels are marks of authenticity, not falsification.

1. The Bible teaches progressive revelation

The concept is not a late Christian invention; It is in the Old Testament itself. The prophets announce a future work of God that will expand previous understanding: Jeremiah proclaims a new covenant in which the law will be written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34) and Ezekiel promises a new heart and the Spirit within man (Ezekiel 36:26–27). The AT, therefore, recognizes itself as a path to greater light.

The New Testament picks up and fulfills that expectation. Hebrews 1:1–2 summarizes: God spoke “many times and in many ways” and “in these last days” he spoke through the Son. Jesus himself claims that his disciples could not yet bear it all, and that the Spirit of truth would guide them into all truth (John 16:12–13).

On the road to Emmaus (Luke 24:26–27), Jesus interprets the Scriptures by showing "what they said about Him." It does not impose an alien reading; reveals the meaning intended by God that comes to its fullness in Him. To reject the Christological reading of the OT because "it was not understood that way by all the ancients" is to ignore the internal principle of the Bible: revelation advances in stages and culminates in Christ.

2. The oldest testimony: "Jesus is the Christ"

Pre-Pauline tradition (30–40 AD). Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15:3–4 a formula of faith he received: "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures; he was buried and rose again on the third day." This confession emerges a few years after the crucifixion. There is no historical window sufficient for an elaborate myth.

Pauline letters (50–60 AD). Romans 1:1–4 and Philippians 2:5–11, among others, identify Jesus as the Messiah and link his death and resurrection to God's plan announced in the OT. These letters precede the writing of the Gospels and already establish the doctrinal core.

Gospels (65–90 AD). The Gospels narratively embody the same message: Jesus, the Messiah, suffers, dies and is resurrected. Four independent accounts coincide in the core and differ in secondary details, as is normal in truthful testimonies.

Apostolic fathers and apologists (110–180 AD). Polycarp, disciple of the apostle John, writes c. 110 AD that "our Lord Jesus Christ suffered for our sins and God raised him from the dead." Irenaeus, a disciple of Polycarp, states that "the prophets announced that the Christ must suffer," applying Isaiah 53 to Jesus. Justin Martyr, around 150, argues with a Jewish interlocutor from Isaiah 53 and Psalm 22 to show that Jesus is the Suffering Servant.

Historical conclusion: From the earliest testimonies we possess, the Church unambiguously confesses that Jesus is the Messiah, and that his suffering and resurrection fulfill the Scriptures. There is no "original Christianity" without this confession: it is original Christianity.

3. Historical reliability of the Gospels: "inconsistencies" as a seal of authenticity

Historical and forensic research applies standard criteria to multiple testimonies: historical intent, capacity and proximity, character (cost of telling the truth), core consistency with peripheral variation, and external corroboration.

  • Historical intention: Luke claims to have investigated “diligently” to write “orderly” (Luke 1:1–4).
  • Capacity and proximity: witness traditions preserved in the first generation; Paul cites formulas before him.
  • Character: the bearers of the message assume persecution; there are no plausible incentives for systematic fraud.
  • Core consistency: public ministry, crucifixion under Pilate, empty tomb and Easter experiences; Differences in detail are normal in independent accounts and discourage the hypothesis of collusion.
  • External corroboration: Roman and Jewish chroniclers, geography, titulatures and archeology place the stories in their real setting.

These differences also produce "undesigned coincidences": one gospel incidentally resolves a question that another leaves open (for example, the mention of Philip in John 6 makes full sense when we learn from Luke 9 that they were near Bethsaida, their city). This type of unplanned fit is a classic mark of testimonial authenticity.

4. Can the Gospels be "proven false" just because they include miracles?

No. Ruling out miracles on principle is naturalistic prejudice, not historical method. History evaluates evidence, it does not decree what can or cannot happen. The burden of proof falls on the person who claims the falsehood. As long as positive evidence of interpolation or fraud is not presented—and it does not exist for the central nuclei—the historicity of the testimonies remains.

Disbelief in miracles is a legitimate philosophical position, but it does not constitute a historical refutation.

Conclusion

Theologically, the Bible teaches a model of progressive revelation that culminates in Christ; historically, the confession of Jesus as the suffering and resurrected Messiah is present in the earliest testimonies and is transmitted without interruption; methodologically, the variations between the Gospels are compatible with true stories and, in many cases, reinforce their authenticity.

Early Christianity did not rewrite the Old Testament: he read it in light of his fulfillment. The figure of Christ does not distort the biblical story: complete it and explain it. The revelation was not manipulated, but manifested; and its center, from the beginning, was the same: Jesus, the fulfillment of all revelation.
