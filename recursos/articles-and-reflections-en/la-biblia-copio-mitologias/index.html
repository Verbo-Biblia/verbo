"The Bible copied mythologies"?

Why that comparative framework fails, and how to better read the parallels.

Thesis in one sentence: the bible is born in dialogue with its ancient world, but does not depend of their myths: reread and subvert from monotheism, the alliance and the history of redemption. Confuse cultural contact with copy-paste It is a methodological error that the biblical scholar Samuel Sandmel called "parallelomania."

1. Methodology: how to compare without falling into "parallelomania"

Error to disassemble: "If they sound similar, then the biblical text is a copy." Methodological correction (Sandmel): serious comparison requires context, dating, function and theology; It is not enough to juxtapose phrases or images. Sandmel warned that seeing parallels everywhere and turning them into affiliations is an academic vice.

2. Genesis 1 is not "Enuma Elish with changed names"

Frequent statement: "Genesis 1 copies Babylonian cosmogonies." What top-notch studies show: Mark S. Smith (The Priestly Vision of Genesis 1) points out that Genesis 1 distances of the divine combats of the ancient Near East; its focus is the sovereignty of one God, the goodness of order and the human vocation, not a theogony with competing gods. John H. Walton (Ancient Near Eastern Thought and the Old Testament) explains that Israel shares the "cognitive environment" of its time, but it reconfigure: creation as assignment of functions, cosmos as a space of presence for man, not as machinery to nourish the gods.

3. "Baal vs. Yam" ≠ "Yahweh vs. the sea": literary echoes, opposing theology

Leviathan/Rahab and the sea appear in Hebrew poetry as images of chaos (Psalms 74; 89; Isaiah 51). The biblical scholar John Day showed Canaanite echoes in that imagery. But the Old Testament demythologizes and subordinates those figures to Yahweh, the only Creator; there is no theogony or coronation of Baal, but ethical monotheism. They are theological metaphors integrated into the Exodus and creation, not confessions of pagan faith.

4. "Copied Flood" or common ancient memory?

Longman and Walton (The Lost World of the Flood) propose that the biblical story dialogues with surrounding traditions, uses ancient hyperboles and organizes the message as theology of disorder and re-creation, not as a scientific chronicle; Its uniqueness is ethical and pactful. The widespread presence of flood traditions in the ancient world is compatible with a shared historical memory; the technical debate is how and where it happened, not whether the Old Testament "made it up by copying."

5. Old Testament as redemptive polemization, not syncretism

Canonical exegesis (Brevard Childs) remembers that theological meaning matures within the canon and the confession of Israel, integrating history and faith without erasing serious criticism. This approach avoids two reductionisms: turning everything into "copied myth", or ignoring the ancient Near Eastern context.

6. New Testament: "repackaged myth" or historical sources?

Frequent statement: "the gospels are myths remade from mystery cults; Jesus may not have even existed." Three weight counterweights:

  • Genre of the gospels. Richard Burridge argued, in a work that changed the academic consensus, that the gospels correspond to the genre bioi (ancient biography), not a mysterious myth.
  • Early sources and witnesses. 1 Corinthians 15 preserves pre-Pauline tradition about the resurrection; Galatians 1:19 mentions "James, the brother of the Lord"; Josephus (Antiques 20.9.1) and Tacitus (Annals 15.44) anchor Jesus and Christians in the first century. Richard Bauckham maintains the centrality of eyewitnesses in the formation of evangelical traditions.
  • The myth-thesis, refuted by non-believing critics. Bart D. Ehrman (agnostic) and Maurice Casey (non-denominational) criticize the denial of the existence of Jesus as deficient and marginal methodology. Jesus did exist; Comparisons with Greco-Roman mysteries do not explain the origin of Christianity.

7. Why "adding parallels" does not prove religious dependence

Religious historian Jonathan Z. Smith showed that four centuries of hasty comparisons between Christianity and mystery religions often responded to confessional agendas, not controlled data. His methodological lesson also applies to the Old Testament: comparing is not the same as deriving.

8. Positive frame: God redeems cultural language

The Bible takes familiar language—sea, monster, flood, temple, covenant—and reorchestrates it with radical monotheism, history and ethics, and redemption instead of mythic cycles. Walton explains it as "shared cognitive environment" with theological resignification; Smith and Day document poetic echoes, not theological servitudes.

Conclusion for the believer who wants to think rigorously

Yeah: There are echoes and common languages ​​of the ancient Near East. No: That does not authorize saying "the Bible copied myths." The best reading recognizes the context and at the same time the singularity: monotheism, covenant, ethics and history. That does not dissolve historical criticism; I have ordered it.
