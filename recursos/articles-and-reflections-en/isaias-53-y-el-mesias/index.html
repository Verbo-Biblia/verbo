Isaiah 53 and the Messiah: textual, historical and prophetic evidence

One of the most repeated objections by detractors of the Christian faith is that Isaiah 53 does not refer to the Messiah, but to the people of Israel. This statement is usually supported by two pillars: the so-called "multiple Isaiah" theory, according to which the book would have been written by different authors over the centuries; and the claim that the Jews never interpreted Isaiah 53 as a messianic prophecy, but that such a reading would be a later Christian reinterpretation.

However, textual, historical and prophetic evidence shows that both pillars are unsustainable. Far from being a late Christian invention, the messianic reading of Isaiah 53 predates Christianity and was recognized by both ancient Jews and the apostles, who saw in Jesus the literal fulfillment of that prophecy.

1. The unity of Isaiah: a single prophetic message

The Three Isaiah theory—Proto-Isaiah (1-39), Deutero-Isaiah (40-55), and Trito-Isaiah (56-66)—formally emerged in the 18th century, but its roots can be traced to medieval Jewish thinkers, such as Abraham Ibn Ezra (12th century), who noted stylistic and thematic differences between sections of the book. However, neither Ibn Ezra nor the ancient commentators concluded that there were three distinct authors; They simply observed variations that could be explained by the different moments of Isaiah's prophetic ministry or by his poetic structure.

Furthermore, no manuscript evidence supports the multi-author theory. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ), found at Qumran (125–100 BC), contains all 66 chapters of the book in a single continuous manuscript, without signs of division or artificial transitions.

Jesus and the apostles also cite the entire book as the work of a single prophet: Matthew 3:3 quotes Isaiah 40:3 as directly attributing it "to the prophet Isaiah." John 12:38-41 quotes Isaiah 6 and 53 together, stating that they both come from the same Isaiah. The unity of Isaiah is not only literary, but also theological: a single prophetic message that culminates in the revelation of the Servant of Yahweh.

2. Isaiah 53 in Judaism before Christianity

Contrary to the claim that Isaiah 53 was "reinterpreted" by Christians, the messianic interpretation already existed within Second Temple Judaism.

Pre-Christian evidence. The Targum of Isaiah (2nd century BC) says: "Behold, my servant, the Messiah, will prosper; he will be exalted and greatly magnified." This Aramaic translation demonstrates that, before Jesus, the Jews already identified the "servant" as the individual Messiah, not the nation of Israel. Document 4Q541 (Aramaic Testament) from Qumran speaks of a priest who "will atone for all the children of his generation" and who will be "accused of false words." Although it does not directly quote Isaiah 53, it reflects a theology of atoning suffering consistent with the figure of the Suffering Servant.

Later Rabbinic Evidence. After the spread of Christianity, rabbis reinterpreted Isaiah 53 by applying it to "suffering Israel," but still retained traces of the original meaning: the Talmud, Sanhedrin 98b, speaks of the "Messiah son of Joseph (or Ephraim)" who suffers for Israel's sins before the final victory. This tradition arises precisely to reconcile the prophecies of a suffering Messiah (Isaiah 53) with those of a triumphant Messiah (Psalm 2). Rabbi Saadia Gaon (10th century) and Rabbi Moshe Kohen Ibn Crispin (14th century) maintain that Isaiah 53 originally refers to the Messiah, and that its national reinterpretation arose only to counter the Christian reading. Ibn Crispin states it openly: "Those who apply this chapter to Israel have abandoned the knowledge of our ancient teachers."

The Qumran community's commentary (pesher) on Isaiah (in 4Q285 and 11Q13) applies parts of the Suffering Servant to a messianic figure, sometimes called "the Prince of the Congregation," who would suffer and die in an eschatological battle. This confirms that the messianic application of Isaiah 53 existed in sectors of pre-Christian Judaism.

3. The apostles and Jesus: continuity, not reinterpretation

When the New Testament quotes Isaiah 53, it does not impose a new meaning; reveals its historical fulfillment. Jesus himself applied it to himself. In Luke 22:37, he quotes Isaiah 53:12: "For I say to you, that which is written must still be fulfilled in me: And he was numbered with the transgressors." He does not "reinterpret" the text; It is identified as its literal fulfillment.

The apostles confirm that reading. Matthew 8:17 applies Isaiah 53:4 to the healing work of Jesus. Acts 8:30–35 recounts how Philip explains to the Ethiopian eunuch that Isaiah 53 speaks of Christ. 1 Peter 2:24–25 quotes the passage to describe substitutionary atonement: “Who himself bore our sins in his body on the tree…” Apostolic hermeneutics does not invent; exposes.

4. Early dating and historical reliability of the gospels

The interpretation of Jesus' death as a prophetic fulfillment did not emerge centuries later, but from the early years of the church. In 1 Corinthians 15:3–5 (written around 55 AD), Paul cites a creed that predates him: "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures...he was buried and rose again on the third day." Scholars agree that this creed originated between 30 and 40 AD, probably in Jerusalem, meaning that the prophetic interpretation of Isaiah 53 was already alive within the first decade after the crucifixion.

The oldest NT papyri (P52, P66, P75) date to the first half of the 2nd century, just decades after the originals. For its part, the Great Scroll of Isaiah (125 BC) confirms that Isaiah 53 existed centuries before Christ, with the same content that we have today. Christianity did not "adapt" Isaiah 53; Isaiah 53 was already waiting for Christ.

Therefore, the claim that Jesus of Nazareth is the fulfillment of Isaiah 53 does not rest on a spurious reinterpretation, but on a solid historical and textual basis, which converges with the original meaning of the prophecy.
