The sin that no one admits: entertainment as a modern idol

If someone asked us if we have idols, most of us would answer, "No, I only worship God." But the heart is not defined by what we say with our mouth, but by what we we give time, attention, desire and affection. We live in the generation that has had the most access to entertainment in all of human history: screens, platforms, series, music, video games, social networks, infinite information at our fingertips.

The secular world has already begun to admit that something is wrong: mental health problems, digital addictions, inability to concentrate, insomnia, growing anxiety. If even the culture that does not fear God recognizes the harm that too much entertainment is causing, how can the church ignore this issue without examining it in light of Scripture?

1. A culture built on entertainment

Our era turned entertainment into the natural context of life. Resting is no longer simply stopping, but rather "consuming content." Legitimate leisure, which can be healthy and necessary, has been colonized by an industry that competes for every second of our attention.

The result is a paradox: we are saturated with stimuli, but empty of meaning. The modern human being can spend hours scrolling his finger on a screen, but he cannot endure fifteen minutes of silence in prayer. You can watch a series of ten episodes in a row, but struggle to spend thirty minutes listening to the preaching of the Word.

"Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, that you may prove what is that good, acceptable, and perfect will of God." Romans 12:2

When the pattern of our mind is formed more by the flow of content than by the Word of God, it is a matter of time before entertainment goes from being a rest to becoming a idol.

2. From legitimate leisure to hidden idol

The Bible does not condemn rest or enjoyment of life. The problem is not that the believer laughs, plays, sees something pleasant, or enjoys a light moment; the problem is when something good takes a place that doesn't belong.

An idol is not just a statue. It is anything that becomes a rival to God in our hearts:

"Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry." 1 Corinthians 10:14

Entertainment becomes idol when:

  • It governs our time more than God's purpose.
  • It defines our state of mind more than the truth of the gospel.
  • It is the first thing we look for when we wake up and the last thing we see when we sleep.
  • It robs us of our appetite for prayer, Bible reading, and communion with the church.

The problem is not only how much we consume, but where we do it from. A heart that flees from silence, that fears facing God and itself, takes refuge in continuous distraction. There distraction stops being entertainment and becomes spiritual anesthesia.

3. Signs that entertainment has become an idol

It is not about creating legalistic lists, but about recognizing spiritual symptoms.

There is time for everything... except for God. You can find hours for series marathons, video games or social media, but there is "no time" for Bible reading, prayer or service. The problem is not the clock, it is the priority (Ephesians 5:15–16).

The mind can no longer tolerate silence. When the soul has become accustomed to permanent stimuli, the silence becomes unbearable. Praying for five minutes feels heavy, but scrolling on your cell phone for thirty minutes feels "relaxing." The heart was educated to live stimulated, not to live in communion.

The spiritual is perceived as "boring." Congregational worship, preaching, personal Bible reading… it all seems “slow” compared to the speed and addictive design of digital entertainment. The problem then is not that the Bible is boring, but that our sensitivity has been formatted by other types of stimuli.

Entertainment becomes a refuge from all discomfort. Tiredness, sadness, anxiety, loneliness: there is a screen for everything. Instead of learning to pour out our hearts before God, we learn to avoid everything with a click. Entertainment stopped being a pause and became drain.

4. The spiritual consequences for the disciple and the church

A distracted heart does not discern. Stimulus saturation affects our attention span. If we cannot remain attentive to the Word, we will hardly be able to discern God's will in the midst of a noisy world.

A town saturated with content, but poor in conviction. The church can be full of activities, broadcasts, publications and podcasts, and at the same time empty of deep conviction. Consuming Christian content is not the same as be transformed by the truth.

"Of which we have much to say, and difficult to explain, because you have become slow to hear." Hebrews 5:11

A "slow to hear" generation is not necessarily a generation that does not hear preaching, but rather one that cannot pause long enough to let the Word sink in.

Community weakened by digital individualism. Today's entertainment is deeply individual. Each one with its screen, its profile, its algorithm and its bubble. The church, without realizing it, can become a place where people come physically, but live connected to private worlds.

A gospel reshaped by public expectations. When the logic of entertainment dominates, the temptation of the pulpit is to adapt the message so that it "does not bore." Difficult texts are cut, calls to repentance are softened, biblical exposition is replaced with motivational speeches.

"For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine, but having itching ears, they will heap for themselves teachers according to their own lusts." 2 Timothy 4:3

5. What do we do with entertainment? Path of repentance and redemption of leisure

The biblical answer is not to deny the existence of entertainment or create dead regulations, but put God back in the center.

Acknowledge the problem before God. Before making lists of purposes, you have to call things by their name. If entertainment took the place of God, it is not "a bad habit"; it's a idol. And idols are not negotiated: they are torn down.

Review the agenda honestly. It is not about counting minutes as if salvation depended on that, but rather asking ourselves: "What dominates my days?" Rethink schedules, reduce excesses, create concrete spaces for prayer, Bible reading and real communion with the church.

Recover practices that contradict the logic of distraction:

  • Daily moments of silence without a screen.
  • Paused Bible reading, without notifications.
  • Deep conversations face to face, without cell phones at the table.
  • Active participation in the life of the church, not just consumption of broadcasts.

Redeem leisure instead of demonizing it. The problem is not resting, but forgetting God when we rest. We can enjoy music, art, stories, games, and moments of laughter, as long as they do not displace the centrality of Christ or overshadow the voice of the Holy Spirit.

"If then you have been raised with Christ, seek the things above, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God. Set your mind on the things above, not on the things on earth." Colossians 3:1–2

The call is not to a gray and joyless life, but to an ordered joy, where entertainment returns to its place as a servant and stops occupying the place of a master. That's the difference between a holy rest and a soul-stealing distraction.

The world has already begun to admit that excess entertainment is damaging the mind, relationships and health. The church cannot afford to close its eyes. If the Lord is truly our God, entertainment cannot continue to sit silently on the throne of our hearts.
