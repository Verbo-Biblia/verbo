Disconnected desire: when the problem in marriage is not (only) the flesh, but emotional disconnection

A biblical, psychological and biological look—without simplifications—on the loss of desire and emotional confusion in Christian marriage.

I. Introduction: the myth of male desire

For generations, it has been repeated that a man desires with his eyes and a woman with her heart. That seemingly innocent phrase has influenced the way society and, often, the church understand sexuality. But human reality—spiritual, emotional, and biological—is much more complex than that cliché.

man's desire It's not purely physical., although the physical has a role. The male body responds to visual stimuli, but The root of your desire is deeply linked to your mind, your emotions and your soul.. When a relationship becomes cold or distant, when validation, admiration, and tenderness disappear, a man may continue to sincerely love his wife, but feel internally dull.

However, this fading does not mean that your desire has died. Often, the desire is still alive, but it has been disconnected from its emotional source. And what many men interpret as a loss of passion may simply be a loss of deep emotional connection.

"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Matthew 6:21

When the heart distances itself, desire disperses; When the heart reconnects, desire is reborn.

II. The biology of the soul: desire begins in the brain

Sexual desire is not born in the genitals, but in the brain. Different systems converge there: the hypothalamus, which regulates sex hormones (testosterone and estrogen); the limbic system, where emotions live (amygdala, hippocampus, nucleus accumbens); and the prefrontal cortex, which integrates consciousness, morality, and decision-making.

Contemporary neuroscience—especially in the studies of Helen Fisher and Stephanie Cacioppo—has shown that desire and attachment circuits partially overlap. This indicates that, in a correlational (non-deterministic) way, human desire is nourished by emotional connection. Therefore, when the emotional relationship cools, the sexual response also tends to cool, even though the body remains healthy.

In men and women, oxytocin (the attachment hormone) and vasopressin (associated with loyalty) play a crucial role. Testosterone is still important, but it does not operate in a vacuum: it requires an emotional context of closeness and tenderness to fully unfold.

According to correlational findings (Murray et al., 2018), in many men the perception of emotional support and validation is associated with greater sexual satisfaction than hormonal levels alone. This is not a universal law, but it is a frequent pattern observed in long-term couples.

III. When desire does not die, but is disconnected

In many marriages, the desire does not disappear: it simply loses the emotional bridge that supported it. This usually happens gradually, when coexistence becomes filled with criticism, indifference or routine. Then the brain stops associating the partner with pleasure and begins to associate it with tension or defense.

Cortisol (stress hormone) interferes with dopamine (pleasure) and testosterone (sexual energy). The person may love their spouse deeply, but their body does not respond. And this bodily silence is confused with a lack of love, when in reality it is a biological reaction to emotional disconnection.

Even so, not all loss of desire comes from this mechanism. There are other purely medical or psychological causes that must be considered: thyroid or metabolism problems, depression or anxiety, medication side effects, genuine hormonal deficiencies, chronic fatigue or extreme work stress.

Therefore, when a couple goes through a sexual cooldown, You should not immediately blame the other or spiritualize the problem without examination.. Human desire is multicausal. The physical, the emotional and the spiritual are intertwined, but none of them alone explain all of reality.

IV. When faith becomes silent and the body shuts down

In the Christian life, it is common to interpret lack of desire as "attack of the enemy" or "temptation of the flesh." And although the spiritual aspect exists, it is not always the sole cause. Many times what we interpret as spiritual warfare is in part a natural effect of unaddressed emotional disorder.

A couple can be devout, attend church and maintain purity, and yet experience dead intimacy. There is no infidelity, but there is distance. There is no visible sin, but there is emotional dryness.

In that context, the believer can fall into resignation disguised as obedience: "I will not be unfaithful, but I no longer expect to feel." That state is not holiness, it is a numbing of the soul. And while God honors faithfulness, he also calls for full life. The problem is not just carnal, but a faith that does not translate into emotional action.

The fruit of the Spirit—love, patience, kindness, meekness—is not manifested only in the church, but in intimate coexistence. When these fruits are not actively exercised, the relationship may continue, but it loses its joy, tenderness, and desire.

"Faith without works is dead." James 2:17

In marriage, faith without affection also dies.

V. Man also needs emotional connection

For years it was taught that a woman needs to feel loved to desire, but that a man can desire without feeling loved. Science and pastoral experience refute that idea. Man also needs emotional connection to sustain his desire. And this need, far from weakening him, humanizes him.

Correlational studies in attachment psychology (Fisher, Murray, Cacioppo) indicate that, in many men, especially with maturity, sexual motivation increasingly depends on the emotional environment. As time goes by, testosterone is no longer the only driving force: tenderness, peace and admiration become determining factors.

When a man lives under criticism or indifference, his nervous system associates home with stress. Cortisol rises, dopamine falls, and desire recedes. On the contrary, when you feel admired, heard and valued, your brain releases dopamine and oxytocin, and the connection reappears.

The man does not stop desiring his wife because she is less beautiful, but because he feels less seen by her.

In pastoral practice, it is vital to remember that validation It is not emotional manipulation, but food of the bond. Simple phrases like "I admire you," "I like how you look today," "thank you for what you do" are spiritual acts, not empty compliments. They are seeds of tenderness that reactivate the reward circuit and strengthen the sacred bond of marriage.

VI. When external validation is confused with attraction

Here occurs one of the most subtle traps, especially in men of faith. A man who loves his wife, who has no intention of sinning, who even serves in the church, may begin—without realizing it—to experience a kind of “attraction” toward another woman. But what happens is not necessarily sexual desire at its origin, but rather a misinterpreted emotional response.

In everyday life—work, church, a ministry, the neighborhood—it is enough for an external woman to speak to him with admiration, thank him, or simply listen to him with respect, for the man's brain to react. After years of feeling ignored, criticized or underestimated at home, that simple gesture activates a dopaminergic release: the brain registers it as relief, as emotional gratification. And because the circuits of emotional and sexual pleasure overlap, the body translates that relief as desire.

The man thinks: "I am attracted to this woman." But the truth is, you're not longing for a new body, but rather reliving the feeling of being seen, admired, and validated.

That's what confuses him. He feels a "spark" that he hasn't felt in years and concludes—wrongly—that his desire for his wife died or that he "no longer loves her." But that's not the case: your body has only responded to an external source of validation that actually belongs in your own home. It's like a displaced emotional reconnection: legitimate energy misdirected.

It was not desire that changed direction; It was recognition that changed places.

And here is the spiritual danger: when man does not understand this process, he blames himself for feeling, or allows himself to be carried away by that sensation believing that he "discovered something new." In reality, what your body is telling you is: "I need to feel valued again."

That's why, Not every external attraction is a sign of perversion. Sometimes it is a reflection of internal emotional lack. And the enemy knows this: he does not need to create a new desire, he only needs to disconnect man from his legitimate source so that he looks elsewhere for what he lost at home.

This does not excuse infidelity or minimize the seriousness of sin, but it does explain why so many faithful men end up confused by feelings they never sought to have.

Psychologist David Buss explains that infidelity is multifactorial: desire, opportunity, emotional lack, the search for novelty and the moral dimension intervene. Among these factors, the research of Buss and others highlights that the search for personal recognition or validation frequently appears as a significant component, especially when the person feels devaluation within the marriage.

The man does not fall in love with a more beautiful woman, but with a woman who looked at him with the respect that he no longer feels at home. But that "new desire" is not desire: it is a misdirected emotional reconnection.

The answer is not to run away from every woman, but to reconnect within the marriage with that which disconnected it. Return to feeling seen, admired and valued in the place where that validation belongs: the pact with your wife.

VII. Shared responsibility: active love and emotional maturity

This issue cannot be resolved by blaming one person. The loss of desire is not the exclusive fault of the woman who does not validate, nor of the man who does not feel. It is a shared disconnection that both can heal.

He needs to take care of his body, his mind and his spirit: rest, manage stress, reduce exposure to external sexual stimuli, express tenderness and admiration. She needs to recover the kind word, gratitude, empathy, and also take care of her own emotional well-being. Both must learn to communicate without judgment and cultivate the fruit of the Spirit in everyday life.

Desire is not demanded or faked: it is built from respect and connection. It is not a formula ("if you do X you will get Y"), but a process where practical obedience to the love of Christ creates the conditions for desire to flourish. The miracle is not that God increases libido, but that he forms the character that makes desire possible.

VIII. Multicausality and comprehensive care

It is essential to understand that not all sexual disconnection has an emotional or spiritual root. The human body has its own language. A wise approach addresses three levels at the same time:

  • Physical-medical: evaluate hormones (testosterone, thyroid, prolactin); review medications that affect libido (antidepressants, antihypertensives); take care of rest, exercise, nutrition and metabolic health.
  • Psychological: detect depression, anxiety, chronic stress or trauma; address unresolved conflicts or emotional burnout.
  • Relational-spiritual: cultivate communication, empathy and tenderness; pray together, reconcile, serve with humility.

Each couple is unique: the patterns described here are recurring observations, not universal laws. Authentic restoration occurs when all dimensions of the human being are attended to: body, soul and spirit.

IX. When Christ rules character, desire flourishes

Christ not only redeems the soul: he redeems the way of loving. When His character governs daily dealings, the body responds naturally. Not because there was a hormonal miracle, but because love became tangible.

The Spirit produces fruit—love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, faith—and each of those fruits has a real biological reflection: love decreases stress, peace lowers cortisol, patience increases empathy, kindness restores tenderness.

The miracle of sexual restoration is ultimately the miracle of transformed character. "The miracle is not that God increases libido, but that he restores the love that awakens it."

X. Conclusion: desire redeemed

Desire is not the enemy of the spirit; It is part of the redeemed creation. The faithful man who feels cold is not necessarily lacking faith, but perhaps emotionally disconnected and confused. The woman who suffers from her distance should not feel rejected, but rather invited to a shared reconstruction.

The solution is not just to pray more or "try more things in bed", but to get back to what is essential: loving, serving, forgiving and valuing.

When Christ governs the character, the soul reconnects, the body responds, and intimacy ceases to be routine and once again becomes a celebration of the love that remains.

"And the two shall be one flesh." Genesis 2:24

A living flesh, not by instinct, but by communion; a unity that honors God, reconciles the soul and redeems desire.
