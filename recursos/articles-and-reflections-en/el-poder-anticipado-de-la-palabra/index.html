The anticipated power of the Word: a soteriology balanced between sovereignty and freedom

Introduction

Soteriology has been one of the most debated topics in Christian theology, especially around how God's sovereignty and human responsibility are reconciled. From Calvinism, with its emphasis on unconditional election, to Arminianism, which prioritizes the freedom of human beings to respond to God, the tension between both positions has generated centuries of theological reflection.

However, there is a perspective that seeks to harmonize both realities—divine sovereignty and human freedom—through the anticipated and sufficient action of the Word of God. This vision does not require prevenient grace as a theological category. Instead, it affirms that divine revelation in Scripture, as an expression of God's character, has sufficient power to generate saving faith in human beings, respecting their will and responsibility.

In this view, divine election is not arbitrary or random, but is rooted in God's foreknowledge (prognosis):

"You have known my sitting and my rising; you have understood my thoughts from afar. You have searched my walking and my rest, and all my ways are known to you. For the word is not yet on my tongue, and behold, O Lord, you know it all. You surrounded me behind and before, and laid your hand on me. Such knowledge is too marvelous for me; high is it, I cannot comprehend it." Psalm 139:2–6

God knows perfectly well how each individual will respond to His Word. This vision allows apparently conflicting biblical texts to be understood in a coherent and harmonious way, eliminating the forced tension between closed doctrinal systems.

The power of the Word of God

The Word of God has an intrinsic, dynamic and transformative power:

"So shall my word be that goes out of my mouth; it shall not return to me void, without having accomplished what I desire, and accomplished the purpose for which I sent it." Isaiah 55:11

This passage affirms that the Word not only communicates, but acts effectively in those who hear it. Its effectiveness does not depend on human capacity, but it does require an open disposition that God expects from human beings.

"Then a woman named Lydia, a seller of purple, from the city of Thyatira, who worshiped God, was listening; and the Lord opened her heart so that she might pay attention to what Paul was saying." Acts 16:14

"So faith comes from hearing, and hearing from the word of Christ." Romans 10:17

Here it is shown that faith is not an autonomous internal capacity, but is born from the impact of the Word on the human soul. This faith is a divine gift provided through written and proclaimed revelation. It is not the human being who takes the first step towards God, but God who first reveals himself through His Word.

Faith as a conduit of grace

Scripture, living and effective (Hebrews 4:12), is sufficient to produce saving faith. That faith connects us with the grace of God:

"For by grace you have been saved through faith; and this is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God." Ephesians 2:8

Grace, in this view, is not an invisible impulse that precedes human will. God reveals himself through Scripture. Grace is a reflection of His kind, just, loving, and merciful character. It is not a spiritual energy that drives the human being from within, but it is God making himself known through His Word, manifesting the mystery of the Gospel. By listening to it, the human being has the responsibility to attend to it, persevere and, through the Word, God grants saving faith (1 Peter 1:23; James 1:18).

Divine election and God's foreknowledge

One of the pillars of this perspective is the understanding of election based on God's foreknowledge:

"Chosen according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, by the sanctifying work of the Spirit, to obey Jesus Christ..." 1 Peter 1:2

"Christ was known before the foundation of the world, but has appeared in these last times for your sake." 1 Peter 1:20

This shows that election is not detached from time or human history, but is linked to God's knowledge of each personal response to His revelation.

Church Fathers, such as John Chrysostom, taught that divine election is related to what God knows man will freely do in response to His Word. Irenaeus also emphasizes that authentic faith implies a response of the heart and will: it is not just an intellectual assent, but a total surrender to the message of the Gospel. It rejects the idea that God creates some for damnation and others for salvation, affirming that grace is offered to all, respecting human freedom.

The concept of choice based on "foreknowledge" in the New Testament reflects the Jewish view of free will, where human responsibility is real and meaningful, in contrast to Greek determinism.

God's attraction and human response

"And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all to myself." John 12:32

This universal call is not an irresistible imposition, but an open invitation. The attraction of God occurs through the proclamation of the cross, and not everyone responds the same:

"No one can come to me unless the Father draws him." John 6:44

This attraction occurs through the Word: when human beings decide to listen with sincerity and attention, the Holy Spirit acts on their will, producing saving faith. The call of James 4:8 ("Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you") reflects a free response to a divine initiative manifested in the Word.

The harmonization of biblical texts

This perspective does not force the texts to fit into a rigid theological system, but rather shows how each passage fits together naturally:

  • Divine Sovereignty: Ephesians 1:4 — “he chose us…according to the purpose of his will.”
  • Human responsibility: Deuteronomy 30:19 — "Therefore choose life."
  • Universality of the call: 1 Timothy 2:4 — "God wants all men to be saved."
  • Power of the Word: Hebrews 4:12; Romans 10:13–17; 1 Peter 1:23.
  • Grace as revelation: Isaiah 55:6–11; John 1:14.
  • Faith as a gift through the Word: Romans 10:17; Ephesians 2:8–10.

God works through His Word, revealing His character and calling everyone to salvation. The human being can respond to that call, facilitated by the same Word that he hears.

Conclusion

This soteriological perspective offers a balanced view that respects both God's sovereignty and human freedom. It affirms that it is God who takes the first step by revealing himself in His Word, which has a life-giving power that generates faith in those who listen to it with a sincere heart. That faith, granted through the Word, acts as the conduit through which human beings receive God's grace.

There is no need for a mysterious grace prior to human decision, because the Word itself—living, sufficient, inspired—is the means by which God grants His grace. Thus, salvation is neither a divine imposition nor a human conquest, but a sacred interaction: God reveals himself, the human being responds, and the glory remains God's alone.
