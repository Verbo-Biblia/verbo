Prepare the way and prepare the heart

The call of Jesus in Matthew in the light of Scripture and the first centuries.

The accounts of the calling of the disciples in the Gospel according to Matthew do not describe an irresistible divine compulsion, but the free and ready response of hearts previously prepared by the Word. In the scene of the fishermen by the sea (Matthew 4:18–22) and in the call of Matthew, the publican (Matthew 9:9–13), the evangelist presents us with men who, upon hearing the voice of Christ, respond without delay because their interior had already been prepared by the preaching of John the Baptist and by a progressive revelation of the Messiah.

This interpretation not only harmonizes with the whole of Scripture, but also with the understanding of Christians of the first centuries, who emphasized human freedom before grace and the pedagogical process by which teaching prepares the soul for the encounter with the Logos.

The Word that prepares before the manifestation

From the beginning, the Bible shows a divine order: first the Word that awakens and disposes; then the encounter with the Lord. Luke tells us that John came "to prepare a people ready for the Lord" (Luke 1:17). The Greek verb hetoimasai —prepare, arrange—describes internal work, not external programming. The voice of the Precursor called to repentance, straightened the paths of the heart and opened the ground to recognize the One who came after.

Isaiah had anticipated it: "Prepare the way for Yahweh" (Isaiah 40:3), a text that Matthew quotes explicitly (Matthew 3:3). The preparation is moral and spiritual, a rectification of inner paths. In the Gospel of John, this dynamic is made visible: the Baptist "came as a testimony, so that all might believe through him" (John 1:7). Andrew and another disciple, probably John, heard this testimony, recognized the Lamb of God, and led Simon (John 1:41–42). Faith is not born from an irresistible force, but from testimony that moves the heart.

Scripture is consistent: before every decisive encounter with God, there is a word that awakens. Israel had to be sanctified before hearing the voice from Sinai (Exodus 19). Joshua ordered the people to purify themselves before crossing the Jordan (Joshua 3:5). Mark opens his gospel with the same sequence: first John, then Jesus (Mark 1:2–5). Divine pedagogy is constant: the Word prepares, and the willing heart responds.

Fishermen leaving their nets: Matthew 4:18–22

The story begins with a phrase full of dynamism: "εὐθέως ἀφέντες τὰ δίκτυα ἠκολούθησαν αὐτῷ" (eutheōs aphentes ta diktya ēkolouthēsan auto), "immediately, leaving the nets, they followed him." The adverb eutheos It highlights promptness, not irresistibility. The emphasis falls on the authority of Jesus and on the free decision of the disciple who recognizes His voice, as the Lord Himself will later say: "My sheep hear my voice, and follow me" (John 10:27).

The literary and theological context suggests that these men were not facing a stranger. According to the Johannine account (John 1:35–42), Andrew and his brother Simon had already heard the Baptist and had their first contact with Jesus. What happens in Matthew 4 is not a sudden reaction without precedent, but the culmination of a process. The word heard had prepared them; the direct call completed them.

This model reflects the prophetic calls of the Old Testament: Amos, Jeremiah, Isaiah. In all of them, the divine voice breaks through with authority, but the response involves discernment and obedience. Promptness is a sign of living faith, not coercion.

Matthew's call: mercy that invites, not imposes

In Matthew 9:9–13 we find a poignant contrast. Jesus sees Matthew at the tax table, looks at him and says, "Follow me." The text simply adds: "And he arose and followed him." The concision of the phrase contains depth: a sovereign word that summons and a free response that rises from the dust of moral slavery.

Historically, Capernaum was a transit commercial center. Jesus had already begun his ministry in Galilee, his fame had spread (Matthew 4:23–25), and John the Baptist had left his spiritual mark on the region. It is not strange to think that Matthew already knew the testimony of the Precursor or the works of Jesus. The call does not come to an absolute stranger, but to a heart touched by the truth. The passage ends by quoting Hosea 6:6: "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice." Jesus does not program puppets; It summons patients who recognize their need and go to the Doctor. Matthew's response is to enter into mercy.

The Rich Young Man Contrast: Freedom That Can Be Denied

The same voice that calls to the fishermen and the publican speaks to the rich young ruler (Matthew 19:16–22). However, he "went away sad." If the call were irresistible, the contrast would have no pedagogical meaning. Matthew uses this tension as a lesson: following can be refused. Jesus calls, but does not force. Human freedom does not contradict grace; makes it visible.

The vision of the Fathers of the first centuries

The Christians of the second and third centuries did not speak of an "irresistible call." Their language is that of divine persuasion, educated freedom, and soul-building.

Irenaeus of Lyon teaches in Against heresies (IV, 37) that God "exerts no compulsion with respect to man." Freedom, he says, is constitutive of good: the Lord persuades, not imposes.

Origins, in his Commentary on Matthew, describes the pedagogy of the Logos: the soul passes from the elemental to the spiritual through the teaching that prepares the understanding. Christ, he claims, "prepared the disciples" for his glory. There is no magic of the decree, but rather a process of transformation.

Clement of Alexandria sees the saving economy in three stages: the Logos invites faith, morally reforms and perfects. Each step presupposes freedom and willingness.

Ignatius of Antioch, although it does not formulate a theory of freedom, it exhorts us to "run together according to the will of God" (Ephesians, prologue), language of loving adhesion, not coercion.

On the whole, ancient tradition conceives grace as a force that persuades and educates, not one that compels. The human response, free and grateful, is part of the miracle of salvation.

The authority that summons and the freedom that responds

The Gospel of Matthew harmonizes these two poles: the sovereign authority of Christ and the freedom of the man who responds.

  1. Jesus speaks with authority (Matthew 7:28–29); his voice summons.
  2. The previous word—John the Baptist—lays the groundwork (Luke 1:17; John 1:7, 31–37).
  3. Human freedom is confirmed in the texts where the divine will is resisted: "How many times I wanted... and you did not want!" (Matthew 23:37); "You always resist the Holy Spirit" (Acts 7:51); "The Pharisees rejected God's purpose for themselves" (Luke 7:30); "Whoever wants, let him take of the water of life" (Revelation 22:17).
  4. The contrast between those called who obey and those who fall away teaches that grace can be accepted or refused.

The anticipated power of the Word

The economy of the call follows a spiritual order that summarizes all biblical soteriology: the Word precedes (John, Scripture, testimony); the Word disposes (produces conviction, metanoia and hope); Christ calls (the willing heart recognizes the voice); the disciple responds (obedient faith that enters into the life of the Kingdom).

The immediate following is not the effect of an irresistible force, but the mature fruit of a disposition that the Word had already worked. Here what we could call the anticipatory power of the Word is fulfilled: the grace revealed before the encounter, which prepares the soul to say "yes."

Conclusion

The call of Jesus is sovereign in authority, but human in its direction: it is addressed to people with history, with spiritual memory, with seeds of faith planted by the Word. The readiness with which Peter, Andrew, James, John and Matthew follow him does not demonstrate irresistibility, but spiritual maturity: the previous work of God in the heart.

This is how the glory of Christ shines: not in manipulating wills, but in conquering them for the truth. The Word prepares, grace invites, and the soul freely responds. That is the mystery of the call: a sovereignty that does not oppress, a freedom that does not boast, and an encounter where both embrace each other in obedience and love.
