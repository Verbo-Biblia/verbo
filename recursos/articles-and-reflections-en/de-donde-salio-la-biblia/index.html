Where did the Bible come from?

Many claim that "the Catholic Church put together the Bible," but the real story is much deeper and much older. Scripture is born from God's action in history, not from a late council. This article presents, chronologically and precisely, how we came to have the Bible as we know it.

1. Before the Christian canon: the Bible of Jesus and the apostles

1.1. Formation of the Old Testament

The Old Testament emerged within Israel between approximately 1500 and 400 BC, written in Hebrew and Aramaic. Jesus and the apostles already treated these writings as "the Scripture", "the Law and the Prophets".

Between the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC, the Septuagint (LXX), which includes the Hebrew books and other additional writings, today called deuterocanonical (Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Sirach, Wisdom, Baruch and additions to Daniel and Esther).

1.2. Why do evangelicals use 39 books?

The evangelical world adopts the Hebrew canon for historical and theological reasons:

  • Return to the canon used by Judaism, the historical basis of the OT of Jesus and the apostles.
  • The deuterocanonical books are not in Hebrew and were considered of historical, but not normative, value.
  • Some passages teach doctrines that the Protestant tradition considers controversial (for example, prayers for the dead in 2 Maccabees).
  • Formal definition of the Catholic canon at the Council of Trent (1546), after the Reformation.

1.3. The Catholic and Orthodox perspective

The Catholic and Orthodox churches, on the other hand, recognize the deuterocanonical books as a legitimate part of the Old Testament, supported by the fact that the Septuagint - the version most cited by the apostles themselves in the New Testament - already included them, and that several Christian communities of the first centuries read them along with the rest of Scripture. From that perspective, the Council of Trent (1546) did not invent a new canon, but rather confirmed in writing a practice that they consider to have already spread since ancient times, in a similar way to how Hippo and Carthage confirmed the canon of the New Testament.

This article follows the Protestant tradition in its exposition, but it is worth recognizing that the question of which books belong to the Old Testament has more than one serious historical answer, sustained by different Christian communities for centuries.

2. Formation of the New Testament

2.1. Apostolic writings (50–100 AD)

In that century the Gospels, Paul's letters, the general epistles, Acts and Revelation were written. From early on, these texts were copied, circulated, and read in churches along with the OT. Some NT writings are already called “Scripture” in the NT itself (2 Peter 3:15–16).

2.2. Criteria of the Early Church

  • Apostolicity: written by an apostle or a direct collaborator.
  • Antique: coming from the 1st century.
  • Orthodoxy: consistent with apostolic teaching.
  • Universal use: read widely in churches.

23. Historical testimonies before any council

Church Fathers such as Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Athanasius cite, defend and catalog the books that the churches already recognized. The first complete listing of the 27 books of the NT appears in 367 AD. in the Festal Letter of Athanasius. Later, councils such as Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) they only confirm the canon already used.

3. Who decided the NT canon?

It was not a Roman council. The canon arises like this:

  • God inspire.
  • The apostles they write.
  • The churches receive, use and preserve.
  • Councils only ratify an already existing consensus.

The authority of the NT does not come from Rome, but from the apostolic authenticity received since the first century.

4. From scrolls to translations: five key versions

The Septuagint (LXX). Translated into Greek between the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC. Includes the Hebrew OT and the deuterocanonicals. It was the Greek Bible of Hellenistic Judaism and widely used by the first Christians.

The Latin Vulgate. Jerome (4th–5th century) translates into Latin from Hebrew and Greek. It becomes the official Bible of the West for a thousand years.

Wycliffe Bible (1380). First complete Bible in English. Translated from the Vulgate. It circulated in hand-copied manuscripts.

Luther's Bible (1534). Translated from Hebrew and Greek. He positions the deuterocanonicals as "apocryphal": useful, but not inspired.

Reina-Valera Bible (1569–1602). First Protestant Bibles in Spanish, translated from Masoretic Hebrew and the Greek Textus Receptus.

5. Books appreciated by early Christians but not canonical

  • 1 Enoch: cited in Jude 14–15.
  • Ascension of Moses: alluded to in Jude 9.
  • Didache: early Christian teaching, highly respected.
  • The Shepherd of Hermas: included in some ancient codices.
  • 1 Clement, Barnabas, Apocalypse of Peter, among others.

Why weren't they included? Because they lack direct apostolic authorship, have limited regional use, emerge after the 1st century, or do not consistently meet the criteria of orthodoxy and universality.

6. Conclusion

The canon of the New Testament was born from the action of God in history, from the prophets, from Christ, from the apostles and from the Christian communities that received the Scriptures and preserved them. Later councils documented in writing what the Christian communities were already experiencing. The question of whether some council centuries later "put together" the Bible depends on what is meant by canon: for the Hebrew Old Testament and the New Testament, Protestant tradition answers no; The Catholic and Orthodox tradition understands it differently, as explained above.

The Bible is not a conciliar invention. It is God's revelation preserved by His people throughout millennia.
