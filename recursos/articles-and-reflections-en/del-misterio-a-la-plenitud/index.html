From mystery to fullness: the revelation of the Father through the Son

1. The Father in the Old Testament: a real, but veiled, presence

In the Old Testament, God manifests himself with power, justice, and mercy, but he is rarely presented directly as "the Father" in the intimate and personal sense that we see in the New Testament. References to the Father exist, but are not theologically developed:

"Is not He your Father who created you?" Deuteronomy 32:6

"You are our Father, although Abraham does not know us..." Isaiah 63:16

"Do we not all have the same Father? Did not the same God create us?" Malachi 2:10

These mentions are real, but the deep knowledge of the Father as a person is not fully developed or explained. In most supernatural encounters in the OT, it is not the Father who appears directly, but someone sent by Him.

2. The appearances of the Son in the Old Testament: the Angel of Jehovah and the visible presence

On the other hand, the Old Testament records numerous personal, visible and audible manifestations of God that interact with human beings directly. By analyzing these texts in the light of the New Testament, many Christians have understood that these theophanies correspond to the Son, the preincarnate Logos. Examples:

  • Exodus 3:2-6 — "The angel of the Lord appeared to him in a flame of fire...and Moses covered his face because he was afraid to look at God."
  • Genesis 16:7-13 — The angel of the Lord appears to Hagar and she says, "I have seen him who sees me," calling that being God.
  • Genesis 18 — Three men visit Abraham, and one of them speaks as YHWH. The story shows God in visible form, dialoguing face to face with Abraham.
  • Judges 13:21-22 — When the angel of the Lord appears to Manoah and his wife, they say, "We will surely die, because we have seen God."

In all these cases, it is a visible, personal and active manifestation of God who is not the Father, since Jesus says: "no one has ever seen the Father" (John 1:18; 5:37; 6:46), but the Son acting as mediator.

3. The eternal Logos: preparing the way for full revelation

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." John 1:1

"And that Word was made flesh." John 1:14

The Word (Logos), the eternal Son, has been from the beginning the agent of creation, mediator between God and man, and interpreter of the Father's will. Therefore, the personal and visible manifestations of God in the OT correspond to the Son: He makes himself known, while the Father remains unseen.

4. The revelation of the Father in Christ: the climax of redemptive history

The entire Old Testament points toward the fullness of revelation that comes in Jesus. He not only teaches about the Father, but He fully reveals Him, because He is the exact image of His being.

"No one has ever seen God; the only begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has made him known." John 1:18

"He who has seen me has seen the Father." John 14:9

"No one knows the Father except the Son, and he to whom the Son chooses to reveal him." Matthew 11:27

"Who being the brightness of his glory and the express image of his substance..." Hebrews 1:3

"He is the image of the invisible God." Colossians 1:15

Jesus not only speaks in the name of the Father, but represents, manifests and incarnates him. Without the Son, knowledge of the Father remains limited; with Him, it becomes intimate and saving.

5. Conclusion: without Christ, the Father is distant; in Christ, the Father is near

The revelation of God as Father in the Old Testament was real, but general. The manifestation of Christ anticipates his work as mediator, but it is in the incarnation where that revelation becomes concrete, intimate, full and saving.

Without the Son, man sees shadows; with the Son, he sees the light. Without the Son, the Father is an abstract concept; with the Son, the Father becomes Abba. The work of Christ was not only redemptive, but revealing: he came to show us who God truly is, not only as Creator and Judge, but as a loving, just and close Father.
