Is Christmas pagan or not?

Origins, Christian resignification and the biblical criteria for celebrating (or not) with a good conscience.

1. "Pagan" practices that many of us already accept

Birthday (party and cake). In ancient Greece, round cakes with candles were offered to Artemis, goddess of the moon, believing that the smoke carried wishes to heaven. In Rome, banquets and gifts were held in honor of the "genius" or protective spirit of the individual. Today, Christians celebrate birthdays as gratitude for the life that God gives, not as worship of gods. The symbol changed its meaning: adoration became thanksgiving.

Quinceanera. It comes from ancient indigenous rites (Aztec and Mayan) combined with Spanish Catholic traditions that marked the transition from girl to woman. Today, many Christian families redefine it as a public thanksgiving to God for the life and growth of the young woman.

Marriages. The exchange of rings, the veil, flowers and wedding music have diverse origins—some pagan—related to symbols of fertility or eternity. But today, in the Christian context, marriage is a holy covenant, and each element is used as an expression of love and fidelity.

Bonus. It comes from the Roman custom of offering strenae to the goddess Strenia, symbol of good omens. Over time, in the Hispanic world the word "aguinaldo" came to designate Christmas gifts, Christmas carols and the work bonus. Today its meaning is not related to gods, but to God's provision and blessing at the end of the year.

End of the year celebrations. The New Year was instituted by Julius Caesar in 45 BC. and dedicated to the god Janus (Janus), symbol of beginnings and transitions. Today millions celebrate with fireworks, dinners and toasts, without reference to Janus.

A coherence question

It is interesting to note that many celebrations that revolve around celebrating human beings—birthdays, marriages, fifteen years, the end of the year—are accepted with total normality, while the only date that, although it also had pagan roots, was resignified to honor Christ, generates more debate among believers.

It is not a question of judging those who decide not to celebrate it—Romans 14 leaves room for both convictions—but of noting the question worth asking: if the life of man can be celebrated, why would it be surprising to celebrate the Son of Man? If one can sing for human achievements, why is it scandalous to sing for the incarnation of God?

It is not about excusing human customs, but about being consistent: if the Christian can redefine so many celebrations to thank God, why exclude precisely the one that proclaims that God became man? Each one fully convinced in his own mind (Romans 14:5).

2. Days of the week

In the Roman tradition, the days were associated with astro-deities: Monday (Moon), Tuesday (Mars), Wednesday (Mercury), Thursday (Jupiter), Friday (Venus); Saturday comes from Hebrew Shabbat, incorporated into Latin Sabbatum; Sunday, from Latin Dominicus, "day of the Lord." Today we use these names as calendar convention, not as devotion.

3. Months of the year

January comes from the god Janus, guardian of beginnings; March, from Mars, god of war; May, from Maia, goddess of fertility; June, from Juno, goddess of marriage; July and August, of the emperors Julius Caesar and Augustus. The remaining months are derived from numbers (September = seventh, etc.). None of these names turn our calendar into idolatry; are historical references, not acts of worship.

4. And December 25?

The Philócalo Calendar (354 AD) records December 25 as the feast of the Nativity of Christ and also of Natalis Solis Invicti (Unconquered Sun). Some historians maintain that the Church chose that date to redeem a pagan day, while others believe that it was a logical consequence of the Christian calendar (counting nine months from March 25, the traditional date of the Annunciation). In any case, the intention was not to honor the sun, but the Creator of the sun, the true "Sun of righteousness" (Malachi 4:2).

5. Biblical principle: the problem is not the day, but the heart

"One makes a difference between day and day; another judges every day the same. Let each be fully convinced in his own mind." Romans 14:5–6

"Eat everything that is sold in the butcher shop, without asking anything for reasons of conscience." 1 Corinthians 10:25

"Whether you eat or drink, or do anything else, do everything to the glory of God." 1 Corinthians 10:31

The apostle Paul does not command us to flee from paganism, but redeem what was once contaminated. God is not glorified in a church that prohibits more than it loves, but in one that sanctifies time, art and culture by putting them at the service of the Gospel.

Paul's example in Athens

When Paul arrived in Athens (Acts 17:16–34), he found the city filled with statues and altars. Among them, he saw one with the inscription: "To the unknown God." But Paul did not condemn the environment, he used it as a connection point.

"Whom you worship without knowing, I announce to you." Acts 17:23

It did not destroy the culture: redeemed her. He did not reject his language: reinterpreted it to reveal the truth. And from a pagan altar, he announced the eternal God. He expressed that same spirit in his letter:

"I have become as a Jew to the Jews, to win the Jews... to those who are lawless, as if I were lawless... I have become weak to the weak, to win the weak; to all I have become all things, so that by all means I might save some." 1 Corinthians 9:20–22

It is easy, with the distance of centuries, to overlook how bold that gesture was. But what it really did was reflect the heart of Christ, who did not come to condemn the world, but to save it (John 3:17).

True spiritual maturity is not in reject culture, but in discern it and redeem it. Because when the believer stops seeing enemies in everything and begins to see bridges for the Gospel, then live like Paul, and like his Lord.

Conclusion

Christianity was not born to destroy every trace of the world, but to transform it with the light of Christ. Therefore, the problem is not in celebrating Christmas, but in doing it without Christ. And the mistake is not in using a tree or a date, but in forgetting to the God who became flesh.
