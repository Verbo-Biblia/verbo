Was Baruch Spinoza the first to ask about the Bible?

Why we can—and should—question it, for readers who want to strengthen their faith with historical clarity, without naivety.

In modern debate, Spinoza is credited with almost unquestionable authority for having denied the divine inspiration of the Bible and reading it solely as a historical document. However, he was not the first to notice textual tensions or possible additions and redactions, and furthermore his philosophical leap—from "human authors and context" to "there is no divine author"—turns out to be an undemonstrated leap.

The informed believer can recognize the human processes of biblical transmission, without giving up the center: the theological unity that culminates in the resurrected Jesus Christ.

1. Before Spinoza: ancient awareness of "post-mosaic" passages

Rabbinic tradition. The Talmud already discussed the authorship of the last verses of the Pentateuch. In Bavá Batrá 15a it is stated that Joshua wrote the last eight verses of Deuteronomy 34, which reveals that there was already awareness of a post-Mosaic addition without collapsing the Mosaic authorship of the rest.

Ibn Ezra (12th century). Long before Spinoza, Abraham ibn Ezra identified what he called the "secret of the twelve" and listed passages that, in his opinion, could hardly have been written by Moses (e.g., Genesis 12:6; 22:14; Deuteronomy 1:1-5; 3:11; 31:9; 34:1-12). Spinoza knew and quoted ibn Ezra; in fact, some of his arguments were already in medieval circulation.

Ancient Christianity: criticism and comparison. Origen (3rd century) produced the Hexapla, a critical edition in six columns (Hebrew, transliteration, various Greek versions) to detect variants. This was already textual criticism several centuries before Spinoza. Jerome (IV-V century), in the prolegomena of the Vulgate, defended the hebraica veritas, reviewing the Hebrew against the Septuagint and underlining variants and glosses.

Historical conclusion: the existence of variants, additions or redactions was known to Jews and Christians centuries before Spinoza. What was new about him was not so much to notice "problems", but to deny them as divine revelation.

2. What modern criticism did show: ancient textual plurality… and substantive stability

The Dead Sea Scrolls show that between the 3rd centuries B.C. and I AD several textual families circulated (proto-Masoretic, "pre-LXX", etc.). This plurality confirms a dynamic process of transmission, but not a collapse of the message.

The Masora (work of the Masoretes, 7th-10th centuries) added vowels, accents, and marginal notes to record variants without rewriting the Hebrew consonantary. That is to say: critical consciousness combined with fidelity to the text.

Key point: recognizing redaction, transmission and variants does not mean giving up divine inspiration; It means historical honesty and trust that God spoke in the midst of history.

3. What did Spinoza do then?

In his work Tractatus Theologico-Politicus (1670), Spinoza denied the entire Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch and proposed that much of the Old Testament was compiled after the exile by scribes—that is, by men—and that the Bible was to be read "like any other ancient text," without regard to divine inspiration.

Can we question it? Yes. Questioning Spinoza is as legitimate as he questioned faith. And when placed against previous rabbinic and Christian traditions (Talmud, ibn Ezra, Origen, Jerome), it is seen that he did not inaugurate textual criticism, but rather redefined its implications from a rationalist framework.

4. The key historical tension

There is a fundamental historical-theological moment that all believers must recognize: if in the first century the Jewish-Roman religious and political system had been able to refute the proclamation of the resurrection of Christ with a corpse, they would have done so, because the threat was serious. The first Christians claim an empty tomb and appearances of the Risen Lord very soon; This combination explains the sudden emergence of Christianity in Jerusalem, within a hostile context.

This is not a closed absolute argument, but it is significant negative evidence: the absence of the exposed body is part of the historical picture that the Christian faith assumes as real.

5. A faith that is not blind: the unity that converges in Christ

Scripture, read honestly, exhibits a narrative arc that goes from creation-fall-promise-redemption-new creation. That unity converges in Jesus Christ.

Historically, the first Christian generation proclaimed the resurrection very early; The formula in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 preserves an early tradition with witnesses—including Paul himself, to whom he "appeared to more than five hundred brothers"—and is considered by many historians to be very early.

As believers, we can accept that the Bible has human processes of writing, transmission and variants. But that does not mean we lose the central message: Christ was resurrected, and our hope is based on that fact, not on the excuse of human authorship or literary debates.

Conclusion

  • Spinoza is questionable, and questioning him is not disrespectful: it is affirming that faith and intellect can walk together.
  • He was not the first to notice textual tensions (Talmud, ibn Ezra, Origen, Jerome had already done so).
  • It confuses "history/redaction" with "absence of revelation" and reduces prophets and scriptures to sociological phenomena.
  • Modern criticism confirms textual plurality and careful transmission, not chaos or destruction of the message.
  • The Christian center is not a documentary hypothesis, but a historical fact: the resurrection of Jesus.

As believers, we study rigorously and accept historical complexity, but we do not subject our faith to literary debates. Our faith is signed in the risen Christ.
