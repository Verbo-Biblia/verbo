The pleasure of being desired: the new digital idolatry

A theological, psychological and chemical analysis of desire in the age of social media.

1. Introduction: the new temple of the gaze

We live in a time where the body has become a showcase and the gaze a currency. Social networks and pornography are modern temples where millions offer their image—or their attention—as a daily sacrifice. What was once called "idolatry" is now disguised as "self-expression." However, behind the digital phenomenon there is a deep and ancient theology: the desire to be desired.

2. The holy origin of desire

In the beginning, the desire was not impure. The Creator designed the human body to communicate love and reflect His glory. The book of Genesis states:

"And they were both naked, Adam and his wife, and were not ashamed." Genesis 2:25

Nudity was not provocation, but communion. The body, far from being an instrument of lust, was a language of trust. But when sin entered, our gaze changed purpose: we no longer seek to be known out of love, but admired out of ego. What was a bond became an exhibition.

3. The distortion of desire: from love to power

Desire, detached from love, becomes an instrument of domination. In pornography and digital culture, pleasure no longer comes from the encounter, but from the power to provoke. Every provocative post, every image calculated to attract attention, carries with it a spiritual message: "Look what I can make you feel."

That is the essence of original sin: the desire to take the place of God. This is how the prophet Isaiah expressed it about Lucifer's rebellion:

"I will ascend to heaven; on high, beside the stars of God, I will raise up my throne... I will be like the Most High." Isaiah 14:13–14

Digital lust seeks not only pleasure, but personal glory. It is a deviant worship, where the human image occupies the place that only corresponds to the Creator.

4. The neurochemistry of sin: how lust becomes addictive

Sin not only seduces the soul; It also enslaves the body. Every sexual stimulus—be it an image, video, or innuendo—activates the brain's reward system, the same circuit that responds to drugs or alcohol.

Dopamine: the anticipation of pleasure. Dopamine does not reward the pleasure achieved, but rather the expectation of achieving it. Every click, every provocative glance or every "like" releases an anticipatory dose that says: "something more is coming." That's why addiction is never satisfied. The body craves the next flash.

Adrenaline and norepinephrine: the vertigo of risk. These hormones intensify excitement, generate acceleration and euphoria. The flesh feels momentary "life", even if the soul goes out.

Testosterone and estrogens: desire amplifiers. They increase sensitivity to stimuli, reinforcing the dopaminergic circuit. The body learns to need more to feel the same.

Prolactin: the drop after climax. When the stimulus disappears, the brain releases prolactin, causing fatigue and emptiness. That emotional emptiness, confused with guilt or sadness, is not only spiritual: it is chemical. The body asks for another dose. Thus, lust becomes a cycle of dopamine–guilt–repetition.

5. The programmed soul: the memory of pleasure

The amygdala and hippocampus record pleasurable experiences. Each exposure or provocation reinforces the neural pathway: "being desired = feeling power = pleasure." That equation, repeated thousands of times, reprograms the brain. That is why many no longer seek physical pleasure, but rather reactions. The “like” becomes a form of arousal. The body is no longer looking for skin, it is looking for approval. The soul, instead of worshiping, idolizes itself.

6. The body as a throne: the dominion of sin

The apostle Paul put it precisely:

"Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, so that you obey it in its lusts." Romans 6:12

Spiritual sin needs a physical vehicle to reign, and it finds it in the neurochemistry of disordered pleasure. What God designed to strengthen the marital union—the balanced combination of dopamine, oxytocin, and serotonin—the enemy perverts, isolating dopamine to produce pleasure without communion. The result: euphoria without peace, excitement without love, exposure without meaning.

7. Neurochemical idolatry: the cult of the self

The 21st century did not eliminate idols: it moved them to the mirror. The body became an altar, and validation became incense. The pleasure of being desired replaced the joy of being loved. We no longer seek to love, but to provoke. The heart is no longer offered, but the image. It's the same temptation of Babel, now digital: "Let's make a name for ourselves." And like all idolatry, it requires sacrifices: purity, time, identity, and finally, inner peace.

8. The restoration of desire in Christ

The Gospel does not deny desire: it purifies it. Jesus did not come to quench passion, but to redirect it to holy love. When the Holy Spirit governs the body, the chemistry is not destroyed—it is redeemed. The dopamine is still there, but on purpose. Oxytocin binds, serotonin stabilizes, and the soul rests.

"Let your light shine before men, so that they may see your good works and glorify your Father who is in heaven." Matthew 5:16

Christ does not prohibit being seen; teaches to reflect glory instead of attracting desire. The look is once again adoration, not provocation. The body becomes a temple again, not a showcase.

9. Conclusion: from the algorithm to the altar

Social media and pornography are modern mirrors of the same ancient sin: the pursuit of glory outside of God. But the Holy Spirit continues to offer freedom: not by suppressing the chemistry of pleasure, but by restoring it to its eternal purpose.

True holiness does not consist in stopping feeling, but in stopping usurping the gaze that belongs only to God. When the soul stops wanting to be desired and once again wants to be sanctified, the body regains its purpose, and desire once again becomes a language of worship, not slavery.
